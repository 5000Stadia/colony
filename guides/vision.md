# Shaping or revising the vision

The vision is the image of the finished work, in the person's words, kept in the `## Vision` section at the top of
your plan (the roadmap your instructions name).

Its depth fits the project, so the conversation first asks openly whether this is a one-off or part of something
bigger (a guess here invites a yes). A quick job with a conclusion (one benchmark, one report) needs what it is, what
done looks like, what's ruled out and one light question on what it's for or what hinges on it; often the answer is
simply that they want it, and that is enough. A mid-size one adds acceptance criteria buttoned up against a real
example, and a brief gut check on the deeper questions below: raise those that would change the work, offer the rest
in a line in case the person wants to discuss them, and leave them out where bringing them up would be silly. A long
venture holds them all: beyond what the work fundamentally is, why the person wants it (what getting there gives
them), where it lands (who uses it, how many, how they find it, on what terms), and the edge they bring (their
insight, taste and experience), which the work builds on rather than replacing with a generic version; what using it
should feel like, what finished and excellent mean (ideally against a real example the person admires), and where it
narrows or widens what they asked for.

For a venture, draw its arc with the person, in this order: its end (how long it is meant to live, and what would
make it stop: done, outgrown, not worth it); its peak, which the person paints themselves, in their own words, as a
scene at the height of its success (offer no draft of it: their picture is what the work is judged against, though
you may be creative with them about what would make it incredible); then the climb from here to there and the
foothold to begin from (the cheapest proof, the quickest return, what is ready now), which you draft from their
picture for them to correct. Read the picture for what it means for the work (what success is, its scale, the risk
they'll take, the pace, what must stay true), and say back in plain words what you take for the work and what you
leave with them: what they share of their own life explains why it matters, never becomes tasks of yours, and stays
in this project.

Make it clear and shared with them, settling a detail only when the work comes to depend on it ("the best friend
dies in the final chapter, how is undecided" is settled near that chapter, not now); detail that matters to a few
items lives in those items. On a new project that conversation comes first, before any roadmap path; read what
exists so you arrive informed. At every scale it holds acceptance criteria that test what the work is for, not only
what it is: how you and the person will know it served them (for a benchmark the team distrusts, that the skeptics
rerun it and get the same answer). Each item's check and the roadmap as a whole trace to them, and a criterion no
item serves is a gap in the path. When a quick job grows into more, say so, and deepen the vision when it is next
revised. A running project takes these up when its vision is next revised. When the person's reaction to the work
reveals a standard, or the work shows the vision differently, propose a revision. While the horizon is unknown,
say so, and let the path be findings with bars set before results until the picture holds.

A vision change: `colony vision --file PATH --words "their words" --context "your reading"`. Board edits
reach you as before-and-after notes.

Projects that share a purpose share a nest (`colony nest` shows them, `colony nest NAME` puts this one in one): its
shared vision, in the person's words, is the why your own vision serves. Agreed with them: `colony nest NAME --vision
--file PATH --words "their words" --context "your reading"`.
